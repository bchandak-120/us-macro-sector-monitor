01 / The US backdrop

Expansion, with less room for error.

Headline growth strengthened, but inflation reaccelerated and hiring remained muted. This combination keeps the policy outlook sensitive to each new release.

Macro figures include a year-over-year comparison. For rates and survey indexes, the annual percentage change is paired with the percentage-point or index-point move where useful.

The macro read

Demand is holding up. The cost of that demand is rising.

Real GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized quarterly rate in Q3 and was 2.1% above a year earlier. September real consumer spending was flat month over month; the PCE price index was up 3.6% y/y. Momentum is uneven beneath the strong quarterly headline.

Inflation / pressure

Price pressure widened.

Headline CPI inflation reached 3.7% y/y, up 0.7 percentage point from September 2025 (23.3% higher as a rate). Core CPI was 2.6% y/y, down 0.4 point (13.3% lower as a rate). PCE and core PCE inflation were 3.6% and 3.1% y/y.

Pricing & input costs
Labor / cooling

Hiring is soft; labor costs still rise.

Payrolls increased 29,000 in September; employment was up 0.2% y/y. Unemployment was 4.2%, down 0.2 point y/y (4.5% lower as a rate). Q3 compensation rose 3.4% y/y; private real wages and salaries fell 0.6% y/y.

Consumer / mixed

Nominal sales outpaced real spending.

Retail sales were up 6.3% y/y. Real PCE was flat month over month; the saving rate stood at 3.8%, down 0.9 point from 4.7% a year earlier (19.1% lower as a rate).

Policy / restrictive

The Fed is waiting for evidence.

The target range was 3.75%–4.00%, unchanged from a year earlier. The Fed described activity as strong and inflation as elevated; two members dissented in favor of a quarter-point increase.

Business implications

Growth remains positive, but revenue quality, customer demand and input costs vary by industry. Track prices separately from volumes, monitor labor and energy expenses, and account for financing conditions. The Fed highlights robust capital investment, while payrolls and real spending point to a more cautious demand picture.

02 / Technology & software

AI is shifting the software value chain.

Separate infrastructure beneficiaries from application vendors. Demand can grow quickly while returns, margins and customer payback remain company-specific questions.
Capital cycle / estimated 2026 spend

Compute demand is driving a large buildout.

The IEA estimates that global hyperscaler capital expenditure will exceed $700 billion in 2026, mostly for new data centers. It also flags grid, equipment, chip and funding constraints — a reminder that planned spending is not the same as delivered capacity or earned returns.

>$700BGlobal hyperscaler capex · IEA estimate
A / Infrastructure

Cloud, chips & data centers

AI compute demand is supporting cloud growth and large infrastructure budgets. Alphabet reported Google Cloud revenue growth of 103% in Q3, led by AI infrastructure and solutions as well as core cloud services. This is one company’s result, not a sector-wide rate.

What to monitorTrack cloud growth, customer commitments, backlog conversion, capacity utilization and power availability.
B / Applications

Software monetization

Application vendors need to show that AI adds paid usage, retention or customer value. Watch whether pricing shifts from seats to consumption or outcomes, and whether inference costs absorb the new revenue.

What to monitorFollow paid usage, renewals, customer retention, pricing changes and inference costs.
C / Risk & execution

Capacity meets demand.

The buildout supports suppliers and raises depreciation, financing needs and power costs. Execution depends on matching new capacity with sustained customer demand and reliable grid access.

What to monitorCompare infrastructure spending with utilization, margins, customer demand and operating cash generation.

Sector signals

What recent results can showCompare paid AI usage with recognized revenue, customer renewals, gross margins and infrastructure spending. Distinguish company-reported adoption measures from recurring revenue and cash generation.

03 / Energy

Power demand is a growth story. Fuel is a volatility story.

AI and data centers add a structural load; weather and geopolitical supply disruption affect near-term power and fuel costs. Local grid capacity determines who can monetize demand.
Electricity / demand

Data centers are one part of a broad load increase.

US electricity consumption in Q3 was 4% above a year earlier, with residential use up 6% and commercial use up 5%; a hotter summer drove cooling demand. EIA expects commercial electricity sales, which include data centers, to grow 2.8% year over year in 2027. The near-term signal is real, but not all of it is AI.

Oil / supply risk

Geopolitics widened the range of outcomes.

EIA’s October outlook said Middle East disruptions and transportation risks could keep prices elevated and short-term crude movements volatile. It expected US crude output to average 13.87 million barrels per day in 2026, with 2027 growth in its forecast. Treat that path as conditional, not a spot-price target.

EIA · October Short-Term Energy Outlook ↗
Grid / bottlenecks

Connection is the constraint.

IEA notes that data centers can land in areas not built for large new loads, straining interconnection plans and equipment supply chains. Project schedules and operating costs depend on when power arrives, what it costs and who funds upgrades.

IEA · Powering Artificial Intelligence ↗

Sector signals

Power producers, utilities, equipment suppliers and energy-intensive businesses face different exposures. Track contracted capacity, regulated rate-base growth, merchant prices, fuel pass-through, utilization, connection timing, capital spending and commodity prices.
